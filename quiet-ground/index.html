Lie on the floor

Somebody posted this week that the best recovery trick for your nervous system might be the least impressive one. Lie on the floor for ten minutes.

No phone. No music. No guru charging you a hundred bucks to tell you to breathe.

Tried it on the living room floor after supper.

First two minutes I was making a grocery list. Minute three I noticed the ceiling needs paint. Somewhere around minute five my back let go of something it had been holding since about 2009.

The floor does a thing a bed doesn't. It doesn't give. You quit holding yourself up because there's nothing left to hold. The breath drops lower on its own.

I got up slower than I got down. That's how you know it worked.

Ten minutes. Free. The hardest part is feeling stupid for the first two. Get past that and it's the best deal in the house.

Longer out than in

Somebody writing about fighters said something this week that stuck. Between rounds, most fighters just stand there heaving. The smart ones breathe in through the nose and let it out longer than it came in. A free tool, he said, and most never use it on purpose.

I'm not a fighter. But I've had days that felt like twelve rounds by lunch.

Here's what I do now, and it's almost embarrassing how simple it is. In through the nose for four. Out slow for six or eight. That's it. Red lights. On hold. Right before I open an email I already know is going to be bad.

The in-breath is the gas. The out-breath is the brakes. Most of us ride the gas all day and then wonder why we can't stop.

You don't need an app. You don't need a class. You need a nose and about thirty seconds.

Try three right now. Nobody's looking.

Slow on purpose

A post going around this week said going slowly is the secret to a calm nervous system. Four thousand people liked it. I'd bet most of them liked it fast and kept scrolling.

I'm guilty. I eat fast. I drive like the road owes me money. I walk through the grocery store like somebody's timing me. Then I lie down at night and wonder why my head won't shut off.

Here's what I've noticed. Your body doesn't listen to what you tell it. It watches what you do. Rush all day and it figures something's chasing you. Then you tell it to relax at ten o'clock and it doesn't believe a word.

So I've started doing one thing slow on purpose. Not everything. One thing. Coffee in the morning, standing outside, barefoot if the grass isn't frosted, no phone. A long breath out. Watching the light come up over the trees before the day gets its hands on me.

It feels like nothing. Five minutes. But that's the thing about slow. It doesn't announce itself. It just shows up later, at ten o'clock, when your head finally goes quiet and you can't figure out why.

It doesn't know it's Saturday

I read a list of ways to sleep better this week. Magnesium, a cool room, no caffeine after noon, the usual. The one they said had the best evidence was the most boring thing on there.

Get up at the same time every day. Weekends too.

Then this line, which I've been chewing on ever since: your body clock doesn't know it's Saturday.

That got me, because Saturday is exactly when I blow it. Stay up late because I can. Sleep in because I earned it. Then Sunday night I'm staring at the ceiling, and Monday morning feels like a hangover I didn't even get to enjoy.

I always figured sleeping in was catching up. Turns out it's more like flying to another time zone every weekend and wondering why I'm tired.

So I'm trying it. Same alarm, seven days. Not to be tough. Just to quit jet-lagging myself in my own house.

Ask me in a month. If I'm cranky, it's not working. If I'm less cranky, don't tell me. I'll ruin it.

Cold water

I read that cold water on your face can calm you down in a few seconds. Meanwhile most of the advice out there wants ten quiet minutes of meditation.

I'm not against meditation. Good for the folks who can do it. But when I'm really worked up, being told to sit still for ten minutes sounds like being told to hold my breath underwater. My mind isn't going anywhere quiet. It's pacing.

A splash at the kitchen sink, though. That I can manage. No app, no timer, no getting it right.

There's something to be said for help that meets you where you are instead of where you ought to be. A lot of advice is written for a calm person who wants to be calmer. The rest of us need something that works mid-boil.

Sometimes calm is a big practice you build over years. Sometimes it's just cold water and a dish towel.

Manage expectations

I saw somebody announce they were taking a little walk after dinner. Very little, they said. Let's manage expectations.

That's the most honest health plan I've heard in a while.

Everybody wants the big transformation. Ten thousand steps, up at five, a new person by spring. Then February shows up and the whole thing quietly goes in the drawer with the ab roller.

I'd rather hear from the folks walking to the end of the road and back and calling it a win. No announcement, no before picture. They're the ones still doing it in a month, because there was never anything to fail at.

Low expectations get a bad name. But a promise small enough to keep beats a big one you only plan.

A short walk you actually take is a walk. A long one you're going to start Monday is just a nice thought.

Instead of the cigarette

Someone felt it all coming up on them and did box breathing instead of lighting a cigarette. That's the whole story, and it's enough. I'm not going to turn it into a lesson.

Four counts in, four holding, four out, four holding. Or don't count at all. Just notice you reached for air instead of the thing that makes the next hour worse.

Some days I remember. Some days someone cuts me off and I forget I have lungs. The practice is the remembering.

October 3, 2026#

Bare feet

The useful list is short and cheap. Sleep. Bone broth. One Brazil nut. Bare feet on grass. Morning sun on your face. None of that has a commercial. That's why it doesn't trend for long.

I'm in my 50s and the bill for the other diet is already here. I'm not overhauling Tuesday. I'm putting my feet in the yard and calling that a win if I actually do it.
